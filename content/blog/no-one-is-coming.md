The longer we live, and experience the world as adults, we all sooner or later realise something fairly profound, though it happens to each of us at a different age.

We all, some way, somehow, have this epiphanic realisation that our parents didn't know it all. That they were human too, a young couple just figuring it out as they went, and that we are the product of that.

When we're young, we think our parents, or the "adults", have it all figured out. A common sentiment, can't imagine many would disagree. Even as we grow older we carry it with us, this sense that there's an essence to certain people, be it family or friends, that they're the ones who have it all figured out. However, it is equally common for people, as they grow, to realise that nobody does. Not our parents, not the people we looked up to, not even the ones who seemed to glide through all of it. They were making it up as they went, same as we are now.

And if that's true, then the person we've all been quietly waiting on, the one who knows what to do and steps in when things get bad enough, doesn't exist either. No one is coming to save us. Not even our parents, and least of all from ourselves.

Jordan Peterson said something in [a lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iDcOuTdjq8E) a few years ago and I just haven't been able to shake it. He's talking about how life comes with a fair amount of misery built in, the kind nobody gets to dodge, and he asks what it would look like if we simply didn't add to it. His answer is that the worst it could ever be is tragedy, and that tragedy is a long way from hell.

The example he gives has stuck with me ever since. You're at your mother's deathbed, and that's tragedy. Now picture the same bed, the same day, only you and your siblings are at each other's throats around it. That's the difference. The first a family can bear, and it might even pull them closer together. The second, nobody walks away from intact.

Then he turns it into a question, and a fairly uncomfortable one. If everyone you love was gathered around that bed tomorrow, could you manage it? And if the honest answer is no, his advice is to get your life in order, because that day is coming for all of us, ready or not. Where he lands is the part I keep coming back to,

> It really is something to be the reliable person at a funeral.

The useful one. The one whose shoulder people cry on, who can be in that room and be of some help to everyone else in it. He's honest that it takes real toughness, because it means taking a major loss without collapsing under it. And if the person you're losing loves you, that's exactly who they'd want you to be.

He comes back to this idea a lot, and when he wrote it down, in _12 Rules for Life_, he made it your father's funeral specifically. He tells his students to aim to be the person in that room that everyone, in their grief, can rely on. I think the choice of the father matters. For a lot of us he is the last person standing between us and the front of the line, the one we assume will know what to do, and the day he's gone is the day there's nobody left ahead of us. What it's really asking is how you will be on that day.

The longer you sit with it, the less it has to do with funerals at all. Every family, sooner or later, has a hard room of its own, and in every one of them there comes a moment where people look around for the adult, only to find there's nobody older left to look at.

Men tend to get handed this early, usually as an order. Be strong, be the rock. I'd argue it's wider than that though, and that it falls to whoever in the room stops waiting first.

The harder half of it, and the part that's hardest to accept, is that it works the other way round too. Nobody can do it for you. You can be loved completely, by people who would give anything to carry it for you, and still be the only person who can get yourself up. They can sit beside you for as long as it takes, but the getting up was only ever yours.

That sounds bleak, I know. I've come to think it's the opposite, and probably the most freeing thing a person can realise. If somebody is coming, then all we can really do is hold on and wait to be found. If nobody is, we're free to move, and there's nobody left to blame either, which is a relief in its own way. Peterson's way of putting it is that we aren't the chaos, and we aren't the plan we cling to either. We're the thing that confronts whatever gets in the way.

I saw a tweet a while back, and I couldn't tell you who wrote it, that said essentially that life becomes a lot easier when you just start blaming yourself for everything. It's a pretty harsh phrase, and I don't think it's right as it stands. Everything is too much. Take the blame for everything and you've only found another way of people pleasing, letting everyone around you off the hook so that nobody has to be uncomfortable, and too much of anything is going to go wrong eventually.

There's something in it though. Take it as a first move and it holds up. Before we look at anyone else in a situation, we should look at where we were in it, and what we did, or didn't do, that helped it along. Ourselves first, others after. Most of the time there's more of our own hand in it than we'd like, and that's good news in a way, because our own part is the only part we can actually change.

Confucius said as much some two and a half thousand years ago, in the [Analects](https://monadnock.net/confucius/analects-15.html),

> He who requires much from himself and little from others, will keep himself from being the object of resentment.

Hard on ourselves, and pleasant with everyone else. It's a double standard, and a deliberate one. We know our own part well enough to judge it, and we rarely know the half of anyone else's.

So we have to yearn for it, intensely. For the betterment of ourselves, of our lives, and of those we care for. Fiercely.
