The longer we live, and experience the world as adults, we all sooner or later realise something fairly profound, though it happens to each of us at a different age.

Our parents didn't know it all. They were human too, a young couple just figuring it out as they went, and we are the product of that.

When we're young, we think the "adults" have it all figured out. A common sentiment, can't imagine many would disagree. Even as we grow older we carry it with us, this sense that certain people, be it family or friends, are the ones who have it figured out. Then we realise that nobody does. They were making it up as they went, same as we are now.

And if that's true, then the person we've all been quietly waiting on, the one who knows what to do and steps in when things get bad enough, doesn't exist either. No one is coming to save us. Not even our parents, and least of all from ourselves.

Jordan Peterson said something in [a lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iDcOuTdjq8E) a few years ago and I just haven't been able to shake it. Life comes with a fair amount of misery built in, he says, and the worst it ever has to be is tragedy, which is a long way from hell.

His example is your mother's deathbed. That's tragedy. Now picture the same bed, the same day, only you and your siblings are at each other's throats around it. The first a family can bear, and it might even pull them closer together. The second, nobody walks away from intact.

Then he asks the uncomfortable question. If everyone you love was gathered around that bed tomorrow, could you manage it? And if the honest answer is no, get your life in order, because that day is coming for all of us, ready or not.

> It really is something to be the reliable person at a funeral.

The one whose shoulder people cry on. It takes real toughness, because it means taking a major loss without collapsing under it, and if the person you're losing loves you, that's exactly who they'd want you to be.

When he wrote it down, in _12 Rules for Life_, he made it your father's funeral specifically. I think the choice of the father matters. For a lot of us he is the last person standing between us and the front of the line, and the day he's gone is the day there's nobody left ahead of us. What it's really asking is how you will be on that day.

It has less to do with funerals than it sounds. Every family, sooner or later, has a hard room of its own, and in every one of them there comes a moment where people look around for the adult, only to find there's nobody older left to look at.

Men tend to get handed this early, usually as an order. Be strong, be the rock. I'd argue it's wider than that though, and that it falls to whoever in the room stops waiting first.

The hardest part to accept is that it works the other way round too. Nobody can do it for you. You can be loved completely, by people who would give anything to carry it for you, and still be the only person who can get yourself up.

That sounds bleak, I know. I've come to think it's the most freeing thing a person can realise. If somebody is coming, all we can do is hold on and wait to be found. If nobody is, we're free to move, and there's nobody left to blame either.

I saw a tweet a while back, and I couldn't tell you who wrote it, that said life becomes a lot easier when you just start blaming yourself for everything. It's a harsh phrase, and everything is too much. Take the blame for everything and you've only found another way of people pleasing.

As a first move, though, it holds up. Before we look at anyone else in a situation, we should look at what we did, or didn't do, that helped it along. Our own part is the only part we can actually change.

Confucius said as much some two and a half thousand years ago, in the [Analects](https://monadnock.net/confucius/analects-15.html),

> He who requires much from himself and little from others, will keep himself from being the object of resentment.

Hard on ourselves, and pleasant with everyone else.

So we have to yearn for betterment, intensely. Of ourselves, of our lives, and of those we care for. Fiercely.
